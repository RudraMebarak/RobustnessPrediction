Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained significant attention in recent years for their ability to efficiently model and analyze complex relationships between nodes and edges, making them a promising approach for predicting molecular properties. This review aims to compare GNNs with conventional machine learning (ML) methods for molecular property prediction, focusing on performance metrics, limitations, and potential advantages.

GNNs are particularly well-suited for molecular property prediction due to their ability to incorporate the structural information of molecules, which is essential for understanding their behavior. In contrast, conventional ML methods, such as support vector machines (SVMs) and random forests, tend to rely solely on the chemical properties of molecules, ignoring their structural information. GNNs have demonstrated superior performance in predicting molecular properties, including electron densities, molecular orbitals, and molecular energies, compared to ML methods.

One of the primary advantages of GNNs is their ability to capture the spatial and structural relationships between atoms in a molecule. This is achieved through the use of graph convolutional networks (GCNs), which apply graph convolutional filters to the graph data, allowing for the analysis of both local and global features. GNNs have also been shown to be more robust to noise and outliers, as they can handle missing or incomplete data, which is common in molecular data.

However, GNNs also have several limitations. One of the main challenges is the computational complexity of training GNNs, which can be prohibitively expensive for large datasets. Additionally, the choice of graph structure and graph convolutional filters can significantly impact the performance of the model, making it essential to carefully design the architecture.

Conventional ML methods, on the other hand, have their own strengths and weaknesses. ML methods are often less computationally intensive than GNNs and can be more straightforward to implement. However, they tend to rely solely on the chemical properties of molecules, which can lead also have limitations. One major limitation is the need for large amounts of data, which can be a challenge for small molecules. Additionally, GNNs can be computationally expensive, particularly for large graphs. Another limitation is the difficulty in interpreting the results, as the models can be complex and difficult to understand.

In contrast, traditional machine learning methods like SVMs and random forests are often more computationally efficient and require less data. However, they may not achieve the same level of performance as GNNs. For example, a study by Kwon et al. (2020) demonstrated that random forests achieved an MAE of 0.22 Å for bond lengths, which is higher than the MAE achieved by GNNs.

Despite these limitations, GNNs have a number of potential applications in chemistry and materials science. For example, GNNs can be used to predict molecular properties like reactivity, stability, and toxicity, which can be critical for drug discovery and materials design. Additionally, GNNs can be used to predict molecular structures, which can be useful for designing new materials.

In the future, there are several potential directions for GNNs in chemistry and materials science. One direction is to develop more efficient algorithms for training GNNs, such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and graph neural fields (GNEFs). Another direction is to develop more interpretable models, such as attention-based GNNs and causal GNNs. Finally, there is a need to develop more large-scale datasets for training GNNs, which can be challenging due to the complexity and size of the molecular graphs.

In conclusion, graph neural networks have shown great promise in molecular property prediction, offering a more flexible and interpretable approach compared to traditional machine learning methods. However, there are also limitations to GNNs, including the need for large amounts of data and computational expense. Despite these limitations, GNNs have a number of potential applications in chemistry and materials science, and there are several directions for future research to improve their performance and interpretability.
