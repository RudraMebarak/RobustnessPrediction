Internal validity refers to the extent to which the findings of a study can be attributed to the independent variable (IV) and not to extraneous or alternative explanations. In other words, if a study demonstrates a relationship between two variables, internal validity ensures that this relationship is not due to other factors that were not controlled for in the study. For example, if a researcher finds that students who take a certain math course (IV) score higher on a standardized math test (DV), the internal validity of the study depends on whether other factors, such as socioeconomic status or prior math knowledge, were not influencing the results. 

External validity, on the other hand, refers to the extent to which the findings of a study can be generalized to the broader population or real-world setting. In other words, if a study demonstrates a relationship between two variables, external validity ensures that this relationship is not unique to the specific group of participants studied but can be applied to other groups or settings. For example, if a researcher finds that students who take a certain math course score higher on a standardized math test, the external validity of the study depends on whether the findings can be generalized to students in different countries, with different educational backgrounds, or with different prior math knowledge.

To further illustrate this distinction, imagine a researcher who wants to investigate the effect of a new medication on blood pressure. The researcher selects a sample of patients and administers the medication, then measures their blood pressure over a period of time. 

Internal validity would be high if the researcher can demonstrate that the effect of the medication on blood pressure is not due to other factors, such as changes in diet or overall health. For example, if the researcher controls for these factors and still finds a significant effect of the medication, it suggests that the effect is likely to be due to the medication itself.

External validity would be high if the researcher can demonstrate that the findings can be generalized to the broader population or real-world setting. For example, if the researcher selects patients who are representative of the general population in terms of age, gender, and other relevant factors, and still finds a significant effect of the medication, it suggests that the findings can be generalized to the population as a whole.

However, if the researcher selects patients who are not representative of the general population, or if they do not control for other factors that might influence the results, the internal validity of the study could be low. To increase both internal and external validity, the researcher could use a variety of techniques, such as:

* Using a random sample of patients to ensure that the sample is representative of the general population
* Controlling for other factors that might influence the results, such as changes in diet or overall health
* Using a double-blind study design to minimize bias
* Using a large enough sample size to detect significant effects
* Using statistical tests to determine whether the findings are generalizable to the broader population

In conclusion, internal and external validity are both important considerations in empirical research.
