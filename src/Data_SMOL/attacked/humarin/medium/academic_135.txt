The importance of using peer-reviewed publications as a measure of academic research impact is well-known. However, the role of these metrics has come under fire in recent times. This essay will analyze the literature and present arguments for and against it. Ginsparg (1998) and others have called for the use of PRPs as a means of measuring academic research impact, emphasizing the importance of peer-reviewed journals in this area over other forms of measurement. However, they have also suggested that these metrics may not accurately reflect the full range of research outcomes, and have cautioned that the current metrics do not adequately address the challenges of using them as an exclusive measure. An alternative metric is the h-index, which measures a researcher's productivity and citation impact. This is accomplished by calculating the number of published papers and the count of cited papers. A higher p value implies he or she has produced high-quality research (Kumar and Jain, 2011). However, there are limitations to this measure. While it may be based on statistical methods, the emphasis on publication/citation counts may not reflect reality.
