The role of synchronous and asynchronous instruction in higher education is explored through a meta-analysis of 20 studies. Tengiz Shengelia conducted this study and found that asymmetric and synchronous instruction had a positive impact on student learning, with no significant differences between the two methods. Sarah Thompson from SUNY studied 21 research articles, while John Johnson used 162 models to compare their effectiveness. The data demonstrates the benefits and drawbacks of using synchronous and asynchronous instructional methods. Asynchronous instruction can provide more opportunities for student engagement and may involve limited resources, such as real-life interactions and virtual office hours, while synchronous instruction often requires less resources or physical time. The authors conclude that these studies highlight the strengths and weaknesses of their respective approaches. These studies also include analyses of the academic record in two academic journals. Doe's research on online courses focused on the impact of synchronous and synchronous delivery. Synchronous instruction was found to be more effective in encouraging active learning and facilitating peer interaction, while asynchronous instruction offered flexibility and greater effectiveness. Johnson's study examined the advantages and disadvantages of asynchron and synchronized instructional delivery in higher education. Synsynchronous instruction provided opportunities for real-time interaction and instant feedback, while others offered flexible and convenient methods. However, adisproportionate approach also had negative consequences such as potential procrastination and reduced student engagement.
