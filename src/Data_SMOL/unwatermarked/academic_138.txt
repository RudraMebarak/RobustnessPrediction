Universal basic income (UBI) is a policy that provides every citizen with a regular, unconditional sum of money, regardless of their employment status. The idea behind UBI is to ensure that everyone has a basic level of financial security, which could help mitigate the negative impacts of automation on workers.

There are several reasons why UBI could be a viable response to labor-market disruption caused by automation:

1. **Financial security**: UBI provides a safety net that can help workers who have lost their jobs due to automation to maintain their standard of living. This could reduce poverty and inequality, which are often exacerbated by automation.
2. **Reduced labor market friction**: UBI could reduce the friction in the labor market by providing workers with a basic income that they can rely on, even if they are not working. This could lead to a more flexible labor market, where workers are more likely to take on part-time or temporary jobs, or to pursue entrepreneurial ventures.
3. **Increased economic mobility**: UBI could provide a catalyst for economic mobility by giving workers the financial security to take risks and pursue new opportunities. This could lead to the creation of new industries and jobs that are not reliant on automation.
4. **Reduced pressure on social services**: UBI could reduce the pressure on social services, such as unemployment benefits and healthcare, by providing workers with a basic income that they can rely on.

However, there are also several challenges associated with implementing UBI:

1. **Cost**: UBI would require significant funding, which could be difficult to finance, especially if it is implemented at a national level.
2. **Administration**: UBI would require a significant administrative burden, including the design and implementation of a benefits system, as well as the management of a large number of recipients.
3. **Effectiveness**: The effectiveness of UBI in reducing poverty and inequality is not yet clear, and it is possible that other policies, such as education and training programs, could be more effective in addressing the challenges posed by automation.
4. **Incentives**: UBI could create perverse incentives, such as encouraging people to stop working or to pursue low-skilled jobs, which could exacerbate labor-market disruption.

In conclusion, while UBI has the potential to be a viable response to labor-market disruption caused by automation, its effectiveness and feasibility depend on a range of factors, including the design of the program, the level of funding, and the effectiveness of complementary policies. Ultimately, a well-designed UBI could help to mitigate the negative impacts of automation on workers and provide a more stable and equitable economy.

It's also worth noting that UBI is not a panacea for the challenges posed by automation, and it is likely that a combination of policies, including education and training programs, labor market reforms, and social safety nets, will be needed to address the complex issues arising from automation. Additionally, UBI may need to be tailored to different countries and contexts, taking into account factors such as the level of automation, the level of poverty, and the existing social safety nets.

In terms of feasibility, UBI could be implemented in a number of ways, including:

1. **Universal basic income**: Providing every citizen with a regular, unconditional sum of money.
2. **Conditional cash transfers**: Providing cash transfers to specific groups of people, such as low-income families or students.
3. **Employment-based benefits**: Providing benefits to workers who are unemployed or underemployed.
4. **Social insurance**: Providing insurance against unemployment, disability, or old age.

Each of these approaches has its own advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of approach will depend on the specific context and goals of the policy.

In terms of funding, UBI could be financed through a range of sources, including:

1. **Taxation**: Raising taxes on high earners or on capital gains.
2. **Public debt**: Borrowing money from the public or private sector.
3. **Privatization**: Selling off public assets to raise revenue.
4. **Social savings**: Encouraging individuals to save for their retirement or other future needs.

Ultimately, the feasibility of UBI depends on the specific context and goals of the policy, as well as the availability of funding and administrative resources.
