Algorithmic fairness is a critical concept in the field of artificial intelligence and machine learning, aiming to ensure that algorithms do not discriminate against certain groups or individuals. The primary goal of algorithmic fairness is to create systems that treat all individuals equally, regardless of their demographic characteristics, such as race, gender, or socioeconomic status. 

However, achieving algorithmic fairness is not always straightforward, and different fairness criteria can produce conflicting outcomes. For example, consider a hiring algorithm designed to reduce bias in job selection. Two common fairness criteria are statistical parity and equalized odds. 

Statistical parity requires that the algorithm produces the same output for all individuals within a specific demographic group. In other words, the algorithm should not discriminate against any group based on their demographic characteristics. On the other hand, equalized odds requires that the algorithm produces the same output for individuals within a specific demographic group when compared to the overall population.

These two fairness criteria can produce conflicting outcomes, as illustrated by the following example. Suppose we have a dataset of job applicants with demographic information and their job acceptance outcomes. We want to create a hiring algorithm that is both statistically fair and equalized odds fair.

Using statistical parity, we might select a model that has a high accuracy for individuals within the majority demographic group but performs poorly for individuals within the minority demographic group. This model would be statistically fair because it produces the same output for all individuals within the minority demographic group, but it would not be equalized odds fair because it performs poorly for individuals within the minority demographic group compared to the overall population.

On the other hand, using equalized odds, we might select a model that performs well for individuals within the minority demographic group but performs poorly for individuals within the majority demographic group. This model would be equalized odds fair because it produces the same output for individuals within the minority demographic group when compared to the overall population, but it would not be statistically fair because it discriminates against individuals within the majority demographic group.

This example illustrates that different fairness criteria can produce conflicting outcomes, and there is no single "best" fairness criterion. The choice of fairness criterion depends on the specific context and goals of the algorithm. In some cases, statistical parity might be prioritized, while in other cases, equalized odds might be prioritized. Ultimately, the goal of algorithmic fairness is to create systems that are fair and unbiased, but the specific approach to achieving this goal can vary depending on the situation.

Furthermore, the concept of algorithmic fairness is not limited to binary outcomes like job acceptance. It can also be applied to other types of outcomes, such as loan approval or criminal justice decision-making. In each of these cases, different fairness criteria might be more or less relevant, and the choice of fairness criterion should be based on the specific context and goals of the algorithm.
