Photovoltaic (PV) cells, also known as solar panels, are made up of layers of semiconductor materials such as silicon that absorb light from the sun and convert it directly into electricity. The process begins when sunlight strikes a PV cell, causing electrons in the material to be excited or "excited" by the photons of light.

The excited electrons then flow through the material towards an external circuit, where they can generate a voltage and current that can power devices such as lamps, computers, or charging portable electronic devices. This process is called the photoelectric effect, which was discovered by Albert Einstein in 1905.

There are two main types of PV cells: monocrystalline and polycrystalline. Monocrystalline cells are created from single-crystal silicon, while polycrystalline cells are made from multiple crystals. Both types have similar performance characteristics but monocrystalline cells tend to be more efficient at converting sunlight into electricity.

One important thing to note about PV cells is that they require direct exposure to sunlight to function properly. Therefore, they should always be placed in areas with plenty of natural light, such as on rooftops or near windows. Additionally, PV cells are not affected by changes in temperature like other forms of renewable energy, making them a reliable source of power for long periods without interruption.
