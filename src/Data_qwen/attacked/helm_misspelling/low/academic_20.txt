Background:

Air pollutants are invisible microscopic particles that include sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter (PM). They enter our atmosphere via various sources including vehicular emissions from cars, trucks, buses, planes; industrial activities such as coal-fired power plants or factories burning fossil fuels; agricultural practices like plowing fields which release methane and ammonia; and wildfires. These pollutants can travel vast distances by wind currents before depositing in populated areas. When people breathe contaminated air containing these tiny particles, they may experience symptoms ranging from irritation to serious illnesses such as asthma attacks, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer.

The inflammatory cytokine response generated following inhalation of pollutants stimulates increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators in macrophages (a type of white blood cell) resulting in elevated serum C-reactive protein levels (CRP)—an indicator of systemic inflammation.

For instance, decreased levels of interleukin-4, transforming growth factor-beta, and interferon-gamma could indicate dampened immune defense against viruses and bacteria while elevated levels of IL-17 suggest enhanced pathogen clearance capability but also increases risk for allergic reactions when exposed to allergens.

Overall, impaired immune function linked to cumulative air-pollution exposure underscores the importance of integrating prevention and protective measures targeting pollutants alongside immunomodulatory therapies when neccessary to safeguard overall public health and mitigate potential chronic health issues arising from prolonged exposures.
