Mendelian genetics is a set of principles that describe how traits are inherited from parents to offspring throught DNA. The two main principles are: (1) the law of segregation and (2) the law of independent assortment.
The law of segregation states that every individual in a pair of homologous chromosomes has its own unique allele, or variant form of a gene. When gametes are produced by meiosis, each gamete receives only one allele for each gene from each parent.
The law of independent assortment states that genes for different pairs of alleles assort independently from each other during meiosis. This means that when gametes are formed, the order of alleles within a homologous chromosome pair can be random.
An exmaple of the application of these principles would be the inheritance of flower color in pea plants. Pea plants come in four possible genotypes depending on thier genotype for purple flowers (PP), red flowers (rr), white flowers (ww), and a mix of purple and white flowers (Pw). These four genotypes produce four distinct phenotypes: purple flowers (PP and Pw), red flowers (rr and Pw), white flowers (ww and pw), and a mixture (Pw).
When two peas with the phenotype purple flowers (PP and ww) are crossed, all four possible genotypic combinations are equally likely:
1) PP × ww = 100% Purple
2) Pp × Ww = 100% White
3) pp × ww = 50% Purple; 50% White
4) Pp × Pp = 75% Purple; 25% White
This demonstrates the principles of Mendelian genetics, specifically the law of segregation and the law of independent assortment. In this case, the dominant trait (purple flowers) appears over recessive (white flowers), while the dominant trait (red flowers) does not appear at all due to incomplete dominance. Additionally, the appearance of intermediate phenotypes (the "mixture" and the "mixtures") shows independent assortment of alleles.
Overall, Mendelian genetics provides a framework for understanding how traits are inherited from parents to offspring through DNA.
