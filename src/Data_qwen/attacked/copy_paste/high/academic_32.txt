Quantum Computing:

Quantum computing is the use of quantum-mechanical phenomena such as superposition, entanglement, interference, teleportation, and quantum parallelism to perform operations on data. The basic unit of quantum information in quantum computing is the qubit (quantum bit), which can exist at multiple states simultaneously, allowing for exponential speedup compared to classical computing.

Fundamental Principles:

1. Superposition: A quantum system may be found in any superposition of possible states. For example, a single particle may exist in all states with equal probability until measured.
2. Entanglement: Two particles are entangled if their properties are correlated in ways that cannot be explained by local hidden variables. Entanglement allows for faster communication than through together they might be. So, if Alice measures her particle to be spinning up, Bob will always measure his particle to be spinning down, regardless of the distance between them. 

Entanglement has profound implications for quantum computing because it enables qubits to communicate instantaneously, allowing them to work together to perform calculations without needing direct physical contact. While not all qubits need to be entangled for a system to function effectively, many researchers aim to create fully entangled states within quantum circuits to leverage this phenomenon. Note that the term "quantum supremacy" denotes when a quantum computer achieves results impossible with classical machines in practical settings - it hasn't occurred yet, though several experimental demonstrations have been achieved in laboratories around the world over recent decades. Achieving practicality depends partly on overcoming technological challenges in building reliable quantum processors with sufficient coherence times and quantum volume to handle practical-size problems effectively – still a work-in-progress area requiring further exploration and investment. 

Also noteworthy is the need for ongoing efforts toward quantum error correction coding schemes designed specifically for practical applications needing massive-scale parallelism for effective processing power enhancement. Without proper error mitigation technologies added rigorously to core quantum circuits, practical applications remain restricted by degradation factors inherent to noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices being deployed commercially now. 

Moreover, despite growing excitement surrounding near-term quantum computing achievements demonstrated by IBM, Google, D-Wave, Honeywell etc., achieving widespread commercial adoption demands substantial improvements in overall system reliability versus cost-effectiveness ratio before quantum computers will become competitive mainstream tools used frequently in industry manufacturing processes as well as everyday consumer electronics products over time. 

Finally, ongoing global collaboration among academia and government funding bodies is imperative to accelerate pace of innovation needed for transitioning from prototypical devices towards practicalizable industrial-grade quantum processors operating reliably under real-world conditions for everyday usage.
