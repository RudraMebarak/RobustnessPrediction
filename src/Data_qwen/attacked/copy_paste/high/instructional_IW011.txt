Evaluation is crucial in any academic pursuit because it involves comparing information from different sources on your topic, determining which sources are most valuable or useful based on their quality.

### Prerequisites:
1. **Basic Understanding of Academic Assignments:** Familiarity with what constitutes a good thesis statement, research question, hypothesis (in qualitative studies), etc.
2. **Academic Writing Skills:** Basic knowledge of grammar rules and citation styles (APA, MLA).
3. **Critical Thinking Skills:** Ability to distinguish between valid and invalid information; assess reliability and bias.
4. **Research Topic Clarification:** Knowing your specific area of study and potential keywords or terms related to your project.

### Step 1: Select Relevant Keywords
Identify key words and concepts that form the basis of your research. These should be central to your chosen theme, but not so broad as to include irrelevant material. For example, if you're researching “global warming,” use broader keywords like “climate change” and more specific ones such as “oceans rising.”

**Action Items:**
- Conduct preliminary searches using Google Scholar, databases, or library catalogs.
- Use Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) to refine your search criteria (e.g., searching for "human rights AND law").
- Note down the top ten results for each keyword.

### Step 2: Evaluate Potential Sources
Evaluate the sources by considering several aspects:

1. **Author’s Reputation**: Check credentials of authors associated with the source (experienced researchers, professors, or well-known experts).

Step 5: Evaluate the author(s)
Examine each author's background and qualifications related to the topic. Look for authors who have published extensively in this field and whose work is cited frequently by other experts. Assess their expertise level by checking their credentials on publications websites like ResearchGate or Academia.edu.

Step 6: Check the publisher/website
Verify the legitimacy of the publisher or website hosting the article. Ensure it has been reviewed and approved by a professional body or organization known for quality standards in academia. Avoid sites affiliated with commercial entities or those without clear editorial policies.

Step 7: Examine references
Evaluate the number and relevance of the article's citations to other studies. Stronger arguments often rely on multiple supporting pieces of evidence rather than just one source. Be wary of poorly constructed citations which may indicate incomplete research efforts.

Pitfalls to avoid:
- Using unverified or biased sources
- Plagiarizing from others' papers without proper citation
- Relying solely on personal opinions instead of factual evidence 
- Failing to assess the credibility of different types of sources (e.g., primary vs. secondary sources) 

Completion verification:
To ensure thorough evaluation, compare your findings against the required citation style format (APA, MLA, Chicago etc.) and cross-check the references against the bibliography provided by the source itself. Double-check all steps taken during the process for accuracy and completeness.
