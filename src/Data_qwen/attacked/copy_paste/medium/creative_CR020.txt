The mapmaker decides to explore this unique society by observing how the citizens interact with the weather in various locations across the kingdom.
The mapmaker arrives at the northernmost town of the kingdom and finds a peculiar phenomenon occurring near the village square. A small flock of birds takes to the skies every time it starts to rain, but remains on the ground during clear weather. Intrigued, the mapmaker decides to investigate further.

As he moves deeper into the kingdom, he notices that each location is marked by its own unique terrain and climate conditions. Some areas receive heavy rainfall throughout the year, while others remain dry for long periods. Everywhere he goes, he observes people adapting their lifestyle according to the weather patterns.

Upon reaching the capital city, he learns from the local officials that this particular kingdom has been abandoned since ancient times due to a mysterious illness that was caused by the excessive use of water resources over centuries. The population dwindled until eventually becoming extinct as they had no means to sustain themselves without access to clean drinking water.

However, the locals are surprisingly optimistic despite the lack of humans living in their land. They believe that once enough rain returns to replenish their water sources, everything will be fine again. To prove their point, they set up large containers outside where villagers can collect fresh water whenever it showers.

The mapmaker records all observations meticulously, noting down every detail he could gather about both the inhabitants' way of life and the environment surrounding them. He even manages to find some old documents buried underground containing historical accounts about this isolated kingdom.

After months of exploring, the mapmaker finally decides to create a comprehensive map of the kingdom based on the data collected. He uses the information provided by the locals about the ideal rainfall period (usually June to August) to depict this special area on his chart. His final drawing showcases three distinct regions – one designated for agricultural purposes, another focused on wildlife conservation, and the third left untouched by civilization for scientific study.
