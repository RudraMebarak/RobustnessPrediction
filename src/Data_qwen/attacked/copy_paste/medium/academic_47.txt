Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is often cited as the most widely used metric to assess a country's overall standard of living or level of development. However, it has several limitations that make it an unreliable indicator:

1. **Single Focus**: GDP only measures monetary transactions and ignores non-market activities such as unpaid household labor, volunteer work, or cultural activities that are vital for human well-being. This means that many aspects of societal progress, including environmental sustainability, health care improvements, educational advancements, and social welfare programs are not reflected in GDP growth figures.

2. **Short-term Focus**: The concept of "GDP per capita" allows policymakers to focus solely on income levels within their jurisdictions rather than considering the long-term sustainability of resources and their equitable distribution among all members of society. A rising GDP does not necessarily equate to higher quality of life unless other factors like education, healthcare, and job security also improve over time.

3. **Resource Misallocation**: By focusing on economic indicators alone, governments may inadvertently allocate more capital and resources towards industries contributing less to overall welfare but which bring greater short-term benefits, leading to misallocations of scarce resources and potential waste.

4. **Environmental Degradation**: Rapid increases in industrial production can lead to pollution, habitat destruction, climate change, and degradation of ecosystems, affecting future generations' health and well-being without being reflected in current GDP figures.

These limitations highlight why GDP should be viewed primarily as an incomplete indicator. For instance, governments might use it alongside other metrics such as HDI (Human Development Index), UNDP's GPI (Global Prosperity Index), or even specific local indices like the Gini coefficient or the Local Well-Being Index (LWBI).
