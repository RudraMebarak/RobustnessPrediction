There are two main types: non-specific immunity (innate immunity), which comes into play immediately after infection occurs; and adaptive immunity (specific immunity), which develops over time following exposure. Both types work together to keep our bodies safe from harmful invaders.
Non-specific immunity includes physical barriers (skin and mucous membranes), chemical barriers (phagocytes, enzymes), cellular barriers (neutrophils, natural killer cells), and anti-viral compounds (eukaryotic organisms produce many antivirals). Physical and chemical defenses are immediate responses and tend to target all similar invaders, but they do not provide long-term immunity because the invader could develop mutations making the previous immune response less effective.

Adaptive immunity takes longer to develop since it requires the immune cells to learn more about each unique viral threat by first detecting the virus at a molecular level and then using specialized receptors (antibodies) that lock onto only certain viruses. This approach provides much stronger protection compared to non-specific immunity. However, if the immune system ever encounters a pathogen it has never seen before (a 'novel' pathogen), both innate and adaptive defenses would fail miserably until a vaccine is created - i.e., humans would get sick and die quickly without any immunity.

The innate immune system initiates an initial immune response by releasing substances that attract cells, causing fever, inflammation, etc.. Adaptive immunity involves the production of specific antibodies and helper cells in response to a particular antigen. Adaptive immunity does not use memory cells like innate immunity does though, instead it relies solely on developing a strong enough immune response based on one's individual genetics & environment. Both innate and adaptive immunity are crucial for defending oneself against viral infections effectively.
