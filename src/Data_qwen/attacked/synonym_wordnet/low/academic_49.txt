4. **Scientific Evidence Support**: Most major scientific organizations recognize the reality of human-caused climate change and the indigence for drastic reduction in GHG emissions to prevent dangerous consequences such as extreme weather events and rising sea levels.

5. **Credibility Building**: Consensus-building within multilateral forums facilitates establish believability among stakeholders. Countries increasingly view international collaboration necessary to combat the threat posed by climate change.

### Failures and Challenges

1. **Lack of Universal Commitment**: Despite repeated attempts to create comprehensive global treaties, many large emitters remain reluctant to commit to ambitious climate goals. 2. **Non-Participation by Major Economies**: In some cases, specially regarding emerging economies like India and Brazil, there's resistance to stringent emission reduction requirements that might affect economic growth.

3. **Implementation Gaps**: Agreement does not equate to compliance. Several countries fail to achieve their agreed GHG targets even when committed under international frameworks.

4. **Risks to Social Justice and Equity**: The burden of climate action disproportionately falls upon poorer regions, exacerbating income inequality. Disproportionately touched populations may feel marginalized and disadvantaged by climate mitigation efforts focused more heavily on richer nations.

5. **Adaptation vs. Mitigation Dilemma**: There’s ongoing debate about whether adaptation or mitigation should take antecedence, especially in light of long-term sustainability objectives versus immediate environmental protection needs.

6. **Negotiation Backsliding**: Historical patterns show that while initial progress can be seen during summits, negotiations frequently falter after periods of enthusiasm. Diplomatic fatigue also plays a role.
