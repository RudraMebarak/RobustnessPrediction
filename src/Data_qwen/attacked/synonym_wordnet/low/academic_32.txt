Quantum Computing:

Quantum calculation is the use of quantum-mechanical phenomena such as superposition, entanglement, interference, teleportation, and quantum parallelism to perform operations on data. The basic unit of quantum information in quantum computing is the qubit (quantum bit), which can exist at multiple states simultaneously, allowing for exponential acceleration compared to classical computing.

Fundamental Principles:

1. Superposition: A quantum system may be found in any superposition of possible states. For example, a single particle may exist in all states with equal probability until measured.
2. Entanglement: Two particles are entangled if their properties are correlated in ways that cannot be explained by local hidden variables. Entanglement allows for faster communication than through the speed of light.
3. Interference: Similar to classical physics, when wavings interfere they create constructive or destructive interference. In quantum mechanics, interference effects leave from non-commutative operators playacting on provinces that are related via entanglement. 
4. Quantum Parallelism: Multiple quantum states can be treated as separate branches within the like space-time dimension, thus realizing simultaneous computation across many states simultaneously, enabling efficient job solving.
5. Quantum Decoherence: Due to interactions with environmental systems, individual quantum states can quickly decohere into their corresponding classical states, demolishing much of the quantum advantage.

Potential Applications:

1. Cryptography: Quantum computers could break commonly-used cryptographic algorithms, but also provide fundamentally secure encryption methods resistant to future quantum attacks.
2. Optimisation problems: QUBO (Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization) formulation provides a common framework for various practical applications including logistics scheduling, financial portfolio optimization, machine learning models etc., where there are millions or billions of discrete choices.
3. Chemical simulation: Simulating complex chemical reactions would accelerate drug discovery, materials science, and design of renewable DOE sources.
4. Machine Learning: Preparation deep neural networks becomes more efficient due to probabilistic interpretations of weights and biases derived from quantum mechanical theory.
5. AI: Quantum machine learning may improve pattern realization capabilities beyond current limitations imposed by classical computational resources.
6. Simulation: Quantum simulations could model exotic phenomena like Bose-Einstein condensation or topological phase transitions that reject accurate representation by standard methods.

Security Concerns:

The threat from quantum computing lies not just in breaking current encryption schemes but developing quantum-resistant alternatives wholly remains an on-going research agenda. 

To counteract these challenges while maximizing the benefits quantum computing offers requires careful development strategies balancing performance gains against potential vulnerabilities. This includes continued progress towards error correction techniques essential for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing platforms. 

Current restrictions include short coherence times, high error rates, and relatively low gate fidelity making scalability challenging even with large investments in infrastructure. To overcome this issue, improvements must focus on ameliorating physical implementations, developing robust control protocols, optimizing software architectures to achieve higher stability and efficiency in real-world environments. 

In conclusion, while the transition presents significant hurdles currently, the promise of exponentially faster computations makes quantum computing one of today's most promising frontiers in scientific advancement with broad implications across numerous subjects ranging from healthcare diagnostics to national surety defense initiatives. Continued investment in foundational research will be key to realizing the full potential of this revolutionary technology in years ahead.

Achieving practicality depends partly on overcoming technological challenges in building reliable quantum processors with sufficient coherence times and quantum volume to handle practical-size problems effectively – still a work-in-progress area requiring further exploration and investment. 

Also noteworthy is the need for ongoing efforts toward quantum error correction coding schemes contrived specifically for practical applications needing massive-scale parallelism for effective processing power enhancement. Without proper error mitigation engineerings added rigorously to core quantum circuits, practical applications remain restricted by degradation factors inherent to noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices being deployed commercially now. 

Moreover, despite growing excitement surrounding near-term quantum computing achievements demonstrated by IBM, Google, D-Wave, Honeywell etc., achieving widespread commercial adoption demands substantial improvements in overall system reliableness versus cost-effectiveness proportion before quantum computers will become competitive mainstream tools practiced frequently in industry manufacturing processes as well as everyday consumer electronics products over time.
