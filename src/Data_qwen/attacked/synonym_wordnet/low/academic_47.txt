Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is often cited as the most widely used metric to assess a country's overall standard of living or level of development. However, it has several limitations that make it an unreliable indicator:

1. **Single Focus**: GDP only measures pecuniary transactions and ignores non-market activities such as unpaid household labor, volunteer oeuvre, or ethnical activities that are vital for human well-being. This means that many aspects of social progress, including environmental sustainability, health care improvements, educational advancements, and social welfare syllabuses are not contemplated in GDP growth figures.

2. **Short-term Focus**: The concept of "GDP per capita" allows policymakers to focus only on income levels within their jurisdictions rather than considering the long-term sustainability of resources and their equitable distribution among all members of society. A rising GDP does not necessarily equate to higher quality of life unless other factors like education, healthcare, and job security also improve over time.

3. **Resource Misallocation**: By focusing on economic indicators alone, governments may inadvertently allocate more capital and resources towards industries contributing less to overall welfare but which bring capital short-term benefits, leading to misallocations of scarce resources and potential waste.

4. **Environmental Degradation**: Rapid increases in industrial production can lead to pollution, habitat destruction, climate change, and degradation of ecosystems, affecting future generations' health and well-being without being reflected in current GDP figures.

These limitations highlight why GDP should be viewed primarily as an incomplete indicator. For instance, governments might use it aboard former metrics such as HDI (Human Development Index), UNDP's GPI (Global Prosperity Index), or even specific local indices like the Gini coefficient or the Local Well-Being Index (LWBI).

To address these challenges, some countries have started developing composite frameworks integrating multiple dimensions of well-being and sustainability into their national statistical systems. These frameworks admit integrating other key variables like inequality-adjusted GDP, happiness scores, ecosystem services value estimates, and others derived from social surveys and participatory processes. 

Ultimately, the use of comprehensive and disaggregated statistics is essential for creating effective policy responses tailored to the actual needs and aspirations of citizens across various socioeconomic strata.
