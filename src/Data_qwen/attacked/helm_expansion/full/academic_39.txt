Photovoltaic (PV) cells are made up of layers of semiconductor materials, typically silicon, that are doped with impurities to create p-type and n-type semiconductors. When light shines on the PV cell, it excites electrons within the atoms of the semiconductor material. This causes the electrons to move freely as if they were ions - this is called an electric current.
The direction of the electric current depends on whether the semiconductor material is p-type or n-type. In a p-n junction, the movement of electrons creates an electric field that attracts more electrons towards the negatively charged end of the cell, creating positive charges in the other direction. These opposing charges flow through the wires connected to the cell's terminals, generating electricity.
In order for a PV cell to be effective at converting sunlight into usable electricity, there must be sufficient amount of sunlight striking the surface of the cell per second. The efficiency of the cell also depends on its temperature; as the temperature increases, the conductivity of the semiconductor material decreases and so does the efficiency of the cell.
Overall, by harnessing the power of sunlight and utilizing the properties of semiconductor materials to generate an electrical current, PV cells allow us to convert solar energy into clean, renewable energy that can be used to power homes, businesses, and vehicles.

Could you please provide some examples of common types of semiconductor materials used in photovoltaic cells? Photovoltaic cells commonly use three main types of semiconductor materials:

1. Silicon: It has been the most widely-used semiconductor material for decades because it is cheap and abundant. 

2. Cadmium Telluride (CdTe): This material absorbs both visible light and infrared radiation well, making it ideal for applications requiring high-efficiency conversion rates.

3. Copper Indium Gallium Selenide (CIGS): This material produces low-cost thin-film modules that are flexible and easy to manufacture. CIGS technology holds great promise as it allows higher efficiency than traditional crystalline silicon-based PVs.

4. Gallium Arsenide (GaAs): A very efficient material but highly sensitive to temperature changes which limits its practical use as a PV source.

These materials are selected based on their ability to absorb different wavelengths of light and their compatibility with manufacturing processes. Some combination of these materials may be integrated together in multi-junction solar cells to achieve higher efficiencies when converting sunlight into usable electrical energy. 

However, modern research continues to push boundaries by exploring new materials such as Perovskite and Graphene-based cells which hold potential improvements in performance over current technologies. 

Also note that while monocrystalline silicon and polycrystalline silicon are two popularly used forms of silicon, the term "semiconductor" itself does not necessarily distinguish between them regarding quality or purity levels since many monocrystalline wafers are made from polycrystalline silicon. Polycrystalline silicon tends to have less uniformity due to grain boundaries and dislocations compared to monocrystal wafers, hence having lower quality. Monocrystalline silicon wafers are generally preferred for high-performance applications due to superior electronic properties, although polycrystalline silicon offers benefits like reduced costs and better thermal shock resistance. However, advancements continue to improve the overall efficiency and stability of all types of semiconductor materials used in PV cells. 

Remember that the choice of semiconductor material can impact the efficiency, cost, weight, durability, manufacturability, and environmental impact of a photovoltaic system depending on its application and intended use. Therefore, understanding the specific requirements and capabilities associated with each type of semiconductor material becomes essential for engineers and manufacturers to choose the right combination tailored to meet particular needs.

As such, the pursuit of improving PV systems remains an active area of technological innovation.
